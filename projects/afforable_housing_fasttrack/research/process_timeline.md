# What being on the list actually does to a project's timeline

Verified 2026-09-19 against city sources and law-firm analyses of the adopted
rules. This supersedes the prior session's Ballotpedia-derived summary, which
had the shape right but not the vocabulary.

## Terminology the prior notes got wrong

The mechanism is **ELURP** — the Expedited Land Use Review Procedure. The
charter amendment created it generally, for "modest" housing projects and
climate-resilient infrastructure, citywide. The **Affordable Housing Fast
Track (AHFT)** is not a separate process: it is the provision that *expands
ELURP eligibility* to larger projects that would otherwise need full ULURP,
but only inside the twelve listed community districts.

So the list does not create a faster process for those districts. It admits
bigger projects in those districts into a faster process that already exists.
The rules took effect **June 17, 2026**.

## The two clocks

**Standard ULURP** — 60 days community board, 30 days borough president, 60
days City Planning Commission, 50 days City Council, 5 days mayoral review.
**205 days**, which the city itself rounds to "roughly 7 months."

**AHFT / ELURP in a listed district** — 60 days community board and borough
president running *concurrently*, then 30 days for the CPC to hold a hearing
and vote, or 45 days where extensive environmental review is required.
**90–105 days**, about 3 to 3.5 months. Summer applications get extended
community board time.

So the land-use clock roughly halves: **about 100 to 115 days saved**, from
around seven months to around three.

## The part that matters more than the days

City Council review is **eliminated**, not shortened. The City Planning
Commission's decision is final. Holland & Knight put it as a shift "away from
political review and toward planning-based review."

That is the real change, and it is why a district landing on this list is a
political event rather than an administrative one — it is what Bay Ridge's
council member is reacting to by advancing her own rezoning first (see
`research-log.md`). The 3.5 months are a secondary effect.

## The caveat to put on any "twice as fast" claim

**Both clocks start at certification, and neither covers pre-certification.**
The charter sets no deadline for the pre-certification period, where scoping
and the CEQR environmental review happen — an Environmental Assessment
Statement, and where required a draft Environmental Impact Statement, all of
which must be complete before an application can be certified. An applicant
may appeal to the CPC for certification six months after submission, which
tells you how long it routinely runs. A complex EIS can add months or years.

Practitioner estimates for the whole arc — planning, design, assembling a
team, certification, CEQR, then the ULURP clock — run **one and a half to two
years**. The fast track compresses one three-to-seven-month segment of that.

It also changes nothing about DOB permitting, financing or construction.

So: "cuts the public review clock roughly in half, from about seven months to
about three" is defensible. "Halves development time" is not.
