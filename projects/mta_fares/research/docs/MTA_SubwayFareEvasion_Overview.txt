MTA NYCT Subway Fare Evasion 
Overview 
General Description 
The Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) is a public-benefit corporation responsible for public 
transportation in the state of New York serving 12 counties in southeastern New York, along with two 
counties in southwestern Connecticut under contract to the Connecticut Department of Transportation 
(CDOT). The MTA is the largest transportation network in North America. 
Subway service within New York City is operated by MTA New York City Transit (NYCT). 
Fare evasion occurs when passengers enter the subway system from the unpaid to the paid side by 
interacting with fare controls in manners inconsistent with the tariff-in other words, by not paying the 
fare. Understanding subway fare evasion is important for estimating subway ridership and gauging lost 
revenue. The data is used regularly by NYCT’s Office of Management and Budget (OMB), and the 
Division of Operations Planning (OP). 
The fare evasion rate is calculated systemwide and is estimated quarterly for the subway with a margin 
of error. The fare evasion rate is the estimated percentage of riders who illegally enter the system. It is 
estimated using surveys conducted by traffic checkers and by applying stratified sampling.  
The fare evasion rate began being publicly reported in MTA Board meeting materials in April 2019. 
This dataset was published during the first phase of the MTA’s commitment to increasing transparency. 
We continually examine all our published and publishable data with a view to both providing datasets 
that can be effectively utilized by our customers and the public at large, and to providing regular, 
automated updates to these datasets efficiently and sustainably. Consequently, this dataset may be 
restructured and/or combined with other similar datasets in the future. 
Data Collection Methodology 
The fare evasion rate is estimated using a statistical survey, which is a study that obtains or records data 
from a subset of a population in order to estimate attributes of the entire population. A survey is used 
since it is unfeasible to have traffic checkers survey every fare control area in a station, and at all times 
of day. Though the sample only consists of about 0.03 percent of the 2.1 million combinations per 
quarter of unique control areas (970) and unique hours (2,160), the statistical techniques are designed 
to ensure that the sample accurately estimates the fare evasion rate for the whole system. 
Surveys only produce point estimates of the true population value, which is unknowable without doing 
an impossible 100 percent sample, and even then, the results would be subject to data collection errors. 
To address the statistical uncertainty of a sample estimate, a margin of error is calculated using a 
formula based on the type of sample, sample size, and variance within the survey result. The error 
margin is then used to provide a confidence interval around the point estimate – in this case tied to a 95 
percent confidence level. This margin of error conveys how far away the surveyed result is likely to be 
from the true population value given the limited number of samples. 
The surveys that traffic checkers take of fare evasion at selected fare control areas and at specific times 
are designed to be representative of the fare evasion rate for the entire system and at all times of day. 
While checkers will inevitably miss some fare evaders due to the limits of human observers, the fare 
evasion rate should be accurate. 
The current method for measuring fare evasion in the subway was developed with assistance from 
Columbia University data analytics professor Dr. Kitty Kay Chan and the Office of the MTA Inspector 
General and was first used in Q1 2021, with a slight change made in Q4 2023. This method is not apples-
to-apples comparable to the previous rates calculated using the previous methodology, though a 
dramatic change in the estimated rate due to the methodology change was not expected. Since this 
dataset includes data back to Q1 2018, before the change in methodology, this document will detail the 
methodologies for both approaches, note the differences between them, and changes made in 2019 to 
improve the previous approach.  
The fare evasion rate is not reported at the station level given the very limited sample size, and since 
most fare control areas are not surveyed each quarter. 
The current approach uses stratification and weighting since it is believed to produce more robust 
estimates of fare evasion that are less susceptible to the variability of simple random sampling. 
The methodology for the current approach to calculating fare evasion is detailed below: 
Sample Generation 
Obtaining paid entry data 
The first step is to obtain a list of paid entries at each fare control area (FCA), which is a grouping of 
turnstiles, High Entry Exit Turnstiles (HEETS), and service gates, in each hour of each day of the first 
month of the prior quarter for which the sample is to be generated, from ridership data. Before Q3 2023 
this data was provided by the Office of Management and Budget (OMB). A station may have multiple 
FCAs. This data is associated with three day types (Weekday, Saturday, Sunday) to capture different 
ridership patterns on different days of the week. Since ridership is fairly consistent during the week, only 
averaging weekday data, rather than averaging specific days, allows more flexibility for scheduling 
without losing too much accuracy. Since this data will initially include only one record for an average 
weekday, the weekday records will be repeated four additional times for a final population of FCA-hour 
combinations. This will better reflect reality (1 Saturday/1 Sunday/5 Weekdays per week). 
Excluding combinations 
This list of combination of FCA-hour type combinations is then updated for permanent and long-term 
closures of FCAs. FCAs used for special events, and FCA-hour types with zero entries for the full first 
month of the previous quarter are exclude. In addition, combinations between 11 p.m. and 6 a.m. are 
excluded since these hours comprise a very small percentage of overall ridership, and to allow for 
sensible scheduling with our traffic checker department. We believe that little accuracy is sacrificed by 
excluding overnight hours from the sample. For Q1 and Q2 2021, combinations between 10 p.m. and 7 
a.m. were excluded due to the 1 a.m. to 5 a.m. overnight subway closure and a travel buffer to allow 
checkers to get to and from assignments. 
Stratification 
Fare evasion is measured using stratification, which divides the population of FCA-hour combinations 
into subgroups (strata) according to characteristics deemed significant and/or for which separate results 
are desired, which is total paid ridership. A statistical requirement for stratification is that the members 
of each stratum be relatively homogenous. Previous surveys found that fare evasion rates and FCA 
entries during the sample period are inversely related. As such, the strata are based on ridership to 
group FCA-hour units with more similar fare evasion behavior together. 
Since fare evasion is measured using a stratified sample, the FCA-hour type combinations are split 
among six strata based on Automated Fare Collection paid ridership totals from turnstiles, defined as 
consisting of equal proportions of total paid ridership. Ridership is highly variable across stations, and 
within a station-across time and FCAs. By stratifying in this way, more surveys are conducted within low-
ridership parts of the system that have higher variance in measured fare evasion rates, which serves to 
reduce the statistical uncertainty of the overall, system-wide fare evasion estimate. 
Each FCA-hour type combination, which is a sampling unit (also known as a cluster), is assigned to one of 
the six strata based on its total paid entries using the data obtained from ridership data. 
Determining the number of surveys 
The total number of surveys to be completed in a quarter is based initially on typical quarterly 
completion rates and checker availability. Based on past averages, the expected number of completed 
surveys per quarter is 490. Assuming an 80 percent completion rate, the total number of surveys that 
will be chosen for the quarter is 612. Completed surveys are monitored to ensure they are free from 
observational and data entry error.

Survey assignment was updated slightly in Q4 2023 to use disproportionate sampling. This is a type of 
stratified sampling in which the size of the sample from each stratum is not in proportion to the size of 
that stratum in the total population. This method allows an arbitrary number of surveys to be assigned 
to any strata while still generating a statistically valid estimate of fare evasion. 
The use of disproportional sampling was made necessary by the Agency’s need to better understand 
fare evasion at busier stations without requiring additional surveys. Using disproportional sampling, 
busier stations could be more heavily sampled in a manner that further improves confidence in the 
system-wide Fare Evasion estimate, and weekend samples could be reduced to accommodate staff 
limitations without interrupting the statistical validity of the system-wide estimate. 
Randomly choosing a sample 
Each quarter, and for each of the six strata defined in the previous step, a sample is randomly pulled 
from the up-to-date population of FCA-hour type combinations, with a total number of surveys chosen 
corresponding to the number of FCA-hour types assigned to each stratum in the previous step. 
Scheduling tasks 
Schedule surveys 
Operations Planning (OP) schedules surveys to match the samples, given available traffic checker 
resources. Each sample is completed only once per quarter. If the same FCA-hour-weekday combination 
appears multiple times in a sample, it should be done on a different day. 
Reschedule surveys 
OP staff regularly review which surveys were not completed so they can be rescheduled for another 
chance at completion during the quarter. They check that the surveys that are completed are reassigned 
to the same stratum, day, and FCA. 
Completion of surveys 
A group of ten traffic checkers goes to the appropriate FCAs at the assigned times and days and 
complete the surveys. They use an iPad app, or paper if there are technical issues with the device, to 
note how many individuals illegally entered through the fare gates over the course of an hour. Traffic 
checkers record every entry type they notice. The list of types of entries is legal (paid, authorized, school 
groups or children under 44 inches), illegal (High Entry/Exit Turnstile bump, swipe seller or open the 
gate) and questionable entries. Questionable evasion is when it is unclear if the person paid or needed 
to pay. including people entering with a gate key or wearing an official uniform, such as a police officer 
or person wearing an MTA vest. Checkers should survey from unpaid area unless their view is 
obstructed. 
Evasion calculation 
This section provides details on how the systemwide fare evasion estimate has been calculated since Q1 
2021. For details on how fare evasion was calculated between 2018 and Q4 2020, look at the section on 
the previous methodology. 
Survey results assigned to strata 
This first step stopped being done after Q3 2023, to allow for a greater number of surveys to be 
measured at busy station complexes (also known as “disproportionate sampling”). Results were 
assigned to strata based on actual paid and illegal entries for each FCA survey hour, including FCA-hour 
combinations that reported no paid ridership. Since actual ridership for specific FCA-hour combinations 
often differed from the historic ridership for these combinations obtained from OMB, some surveys 
were reassigned to new strata. This process created six strata that were almost equally weighted by 
total entries (paid and unpaid ridership) with no overlap.

Previous methodology 
This section details the methodology used to calculate the fare evasion rate between 2009 and Q4 2020, 
and briefly touches on the approach used before 2009. In Q4 2018, changes were made to how the 
margin of error was calculated, and in July 2019, several modifications were made to the survey process. 
Before 2009 
Before Q1 2021, a different method was used to calculate systemwide fare evasion. This method, first 
used in 2009, replaced the measurement of fare evasion by station agents, who counted evaders for one 
day a month to calculate a systemwide evasion rate. Station agent counts were factored up by 35 
percent to account from undercounting expected by this methodology. The data were not 
independently verifiable. Since station agents had other duties while being in the token booth (selling 
MetroCards, providing rider information), distracted clerks could easily miss a few fare evaders. At the 
same time, with decreasing agent positions, fewer control areas were covered. A 2008 pilot study that 
measured fare evasion at randomly selected fare control areas found that the overall fare evasion rate 
was six times the station agent rate-2.1 percent, as opposed to 0.32 percent. In 2008, a new 
methodology was developed to calculate fare evasion using stratification parameters determined by 
two pilots from that year that found that passenger entry rates and adjacent neighborhood income 
levels correlated most strongly with fare evasion observed. This method began to be formally used in 
2009. 
2009 to Q4 2020 
The sample for fare evasion was based on a random sample of FCA-hour combinations, stratified by 
average hourly ridership. OMB sent OP a file with the average ridership based on a year of data for 
every hour of the day at each FCA, separated into weekday, Saturday, and Sunday categories. The 
combinations were sorted by day type and the number of swipes in descending order. Any rows with 
fewer than 10 entries in an hour and exit-only control areas were removed, as were FCAs used for 
events. 
The total number of rows was then divided by the number of strata to find the number of FCA-hour 
combinations in each stratum. 40 strata were used, which corresponded to 40 buckets. Initially, there 
were two median income brackets by 20 ridership strata. The FCA-hour combinations with highest 
ridership would be in the first bucket, with the next ones in the following bucket, and so on. With each 
row having a bucket number, the order of rows inside a bucket was randomized. Each quarter, a new 
sample was pulled from the buckets, starting with the first unused row. The number of rows pulled from 
each bucket, or strata, is 5 for weekdays, 1 for Saturday, and 1 for Sunday, which added up to 280 
surveys in each sample set for a given quarter (40 each for Saturday and Sunday, and 200 for weekdays), 
with equal representation from each of the buckets. This was designed to ensure there was a mix of high 
and low ridership FCAs in each quarterly sample. Weekday type samples are drawn five times more than 
Saturday or Sunday to keep the sample balanced. 
After the sample set for a given quarter was generated, during the first week weeks of the last month of 
a quarter, an analyst in OP looked through and removed any long-term closures from the sample. 
Surveys were assigned to checkers based on their work schedule. Like the current process, checkers 
measured evasion using categories using tablets, or paper as a backup. However, they did so for 30 
minutes within a 1-hour period they were assigned. A checker was expected to do three checks a day, 
spread across three boroughs when feasible. 
Since checkers observed clusters of riders at selected FCAs in selected hours, the evasion rate was 
calculated as a cluster sample proportion. The sampling unit was a FCA-hour combination, and the 
elementary unit was a person entering the system. Like the current methodology, the evasion rate is 
calculated based on evasion as observed by traffic checkers and paid ridership data during the same 
time period.

Descriptions of methodology changes  
At some point, in an effort to improve accuracy, OP began treating the sampling unit of a FCA-hour as a 
cluster for statistical purposes since it was thought that it would provide a more cost-effective and 
accurate estimate of fare evasion. 
As part of the changes made between Q4 2018 and Q1 2021, areas with fewer than ten swipes per hour 
were no longer excluded from being sampled. In addition, the hours that FCAs were surveyed on 
weekends were adjusted from 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. to being 5 a.m. to 11 p.m.. 
Margin of error change in Q4 2018 
Several years later, OP revised the margin of error calculation for fare evasion as of Q4 2018 from being 
a simple random sample to being treated as a one-stage clustered random sample. Cluster samples 
divide a population into groups, and selects the groups at random, and gathers data from every subject 
within that cluster, unlike stratified random samples, which randomly chooses some subjects from every 
group. Each survey, or FCA-hour combination, would be treated as a cluster, instead of buckets, which 
would be ignored, for the MOE calculation. This change widened the margin of error. 
The change was made since OP had begun to consider that it would be more appropriate to assume that 
the proportion of evaders is clustered rather than uniform, an assumption of the binomial distribution, 
which had been used to represent fare evasion in the methodology. Binomial distributions are used to 
estimate the probable outcomes of a series of events when each event has two possible outcomes. They 
are based on assumptions that are questionable when applied to fare evasion, such as the 
independence of each event, since it is likely that passenger decisions are influenced by the behaviors of 
other passengers. The initial methodology had assumed observations to be independent, even though 
that was not strictly true. In addition, the sampling plan had selected every cluster in the population, 
which is what cluster sampling seeks to avoid, and the formula that had been used also did not account

Process changes in July 2019 
In July 2019, a series of changes was made to address concerns about the process for obtaining the data 
raised during the methodology review with Columbia University and the MTA Office of the Inspector 
General. These changes took effect with the Q3 2019 data and could explain part of the increase in 
reported subway fare evasion-with fewer fare evaders missed. 
The MTAIG had found that only 60 percent of surveys for the six quarters through the end of 2018 were 
completed, and wondered what the impact of the shortfall of completion surveys was on the accuracy 
of the fare evasion calculation. To increase the number of surveys completed, several changes were 
made. Traffic checkers previously had limited availability. Subway traffic checkers were part of the 
Passenger Environment Survey (PES) checker group and spent most of their time there. Traffic checkers 
often could not be substituted for in their absence. A position could not be filled until the following pick 
if a checker left the job or was out-long term; this had a direct impact on the number of completed 
checks. Bus and subway checkers were combined into one dedicated and coordinated unit, increasing 
the number of available checkers, and allowing them to be moved around between modes depending 
on sampling needs. Subway checkers would be taken out of uniform; they previously had been in 
uniform while doing surveys since they also had other responsibilities in the PES group. 
Schedulers would begin to assign checkers to multiple nearby locations, instead of being spread out, 
reducing the proportion of the time that checkers needed to travel between observation locations. The 
requirement that checkers visit three boroughs per day would be relaxed to only require them to visit 
two boroughs a day to improve efficiency. These changes enabled a doubling of observation time from 
30 to 60 minutes. This reduced the needed number of checks to obtain the same level of precision, and 
increased the number of checks performed each month with resources reallocated. 
Categories on the surveys were simplified, eliminating unnecessary distinctions, such as whether 
evasion through a gate was deliberate or opportunistic. This would allow checkers to concentrate more 
on quantifying evasion instead of trying to discern a particular evader’s behavior. 
Previously, at FCAs with multiple exits, checkers were instructed to observe all gates within their 
assigned FCA. However, observing more than one at a time could be challenging at times, meaning they 
could miss many evaders. An expanded list of fare control areas that split out over a hundred additional 
control areas was created. It accounted for recent construction, and feedback from checkers and their 
supervisors. The split-up control areas allowed checkers to monitor smaller areas, and thus be better 
positioned to get better sightlines at stations to collect more accurate data. Finally, checkers were 
trained or retrained on standard practices, the number of supervisors assigned to evasion was increased 
from 1 to 3 (it increased to 4 in September 2019), and automated reports were created to flag results for 
quality assurance. 
Q1 2021 methodology changes 
The Q1 2021 methodology changes were developed with assistance from Columbia University data 
analytics professor Dr. Kitty Kay Chan and the Office of the MTA Inspector General. The change reduced 
the number of strata from 40 to 6. FCA-hour combinations would no longer be divided equally among 
the strata; instead, they would be divided so each strata had equal levels of ridership, meaning there 
would be different numbers of combinations in each stratum. 
While the previous approach used strata to obtain a more accurate sample by ensuring there were 
enough surveys in areas with different levels of ridership by organizing FCA-hour combinations by 
ridership, dividing them equally into 40 strata, and randomly choosing some combinations, they did not 
weigh and average the fare evasion rates of the various strata. With the Q1 2021 changes, the fare 
evasion rate and margin of error would be calculated for each stratum and would then be averaged 
using weights. This change meant that the methodology would no longer consist of a hybrid method, 
which combined elements of different sampling techniques. FCA-hours would be kept as clusters for the 
calculation of the margin of error. Another change is that the ridership data used to assign FCA-hours to 
strata for survey assignment would now be from each hour of each day of the first month of the first 
month of the prior quarter as opposed to being from the previous year of data. 
Statistical and Analytic Issues 
Surveys conducted by traffic checkers using their best judgement to count and classify entries, therefore 
human error may take place. Data is available starting in 2018. Fare evasion was not measured for Q2 
2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic. 
Limitations of Data Use 
There are no limitations on the data at this time.
